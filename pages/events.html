NewsGrant guidance

Youth Jobs Grant: the opportunity is real, the job has to last

The Youth Jobs Grant offers employers up to £3,000 for hiring an eligible 18 to 24-year-old who has been on Universal Credit and out of work for six months or more. The scheme…

Read on LinkedIn
Full details

The Youth Jobs Grant offers employers up to £3,000 for hiring an eligible 18 to 24-year-old who has been on Universal Credit and out of work for six months or more. The scheme is designed to support up to 60,000 job opportunities over three years, and it is open across Great Britain until October 2028.

Payment is staged, not paid up front: £1,800 after six weeks and £1,200 after 18 weeks, once employment and earnings are verified through HMRC. Roles must be genuine paid work of at least 25 hours a week, last at least four months, pay at least the applicable minimum wage, and not replace an existing worker.

For Compass Consult, the grant is a useful way to reduce the upfront cost and perceived risk of recruiting young people who have been out of work for longer. It is not a substitute for job quality. Employers, Jobcentres, employability providers, local government and specialist organisations need to use it to build sustained employment, skills and progression, not just job starts.

NewsYouth services

1,000 new youth workers, and the integration test that follows

The announcement of 1,000 new youth workers across England matters for anyone working in youth employment, employability and skills. The first workers are expected to be in…

Government announcement
Full details

The announcement of 1,000 new youth workers across England matters for anyone working in youth employment, employability and skills. The first workers are expected to be in place by spring 2027, with a focus on community support for young people most at risk of becoming NEET.

Youth employment cannot be addressed through employment services alone. The new workers are expected to sit alongside education, training, health and crime-prevention services, building trusted relationships and connecting young people to sport, arts, mentoring and wider youth provision.

The recruitment drive sits inside a wider package: a £450 million Youth Matters Fund for facilities and activities, 50 Young Futures Hubs, more than £500 million through the National Youth Strategy, and the £820 million Youth Guarantee for 18 to 24-year-olds. That sits next to expanded employment support, the Jobs Guarantee and employer-led routes into work.

The practical test is integration. Local partnerships, clear referral and transition pathways, earlier identification of NEET risk, meaningful employer engagement, and better use of local data all matter more than another set of separate initiatives. Youth Matters was informed by research with more than 14,000 young people. The next phase is not only how many youth workers are recruited, but how well the system around them works.

NEET Trends and Youth Labour Market Discussion

Hosted by the Work Foundation

On 26 February, the Work Foundation and an expert panel reviewed the latest ONS figures on young people not in education, employment or training (NEET), and discussed what this…

  • Online event
Event details
Full details

On 26 February, the Work Foundation and an expert panel reviewed the latest ONS figures on young people not in education, employment or training (NEET), and discussed what this means for young people, local communities, the labour market and Government.

The UK has had a significant population of young people aged 16–24 who are NEET for over 20 years, but the challenge for policymakers has evolved. The number remains stubbornly close to one million — around one in eight young people aged 16–24 — following a large post-pandemic rise.

The discussion also considered the Government’s response, including an £820 million youth participation package and work to investigate root causes of youth unemployment.

Panel: Sharon Thompson FIoL (West Midlands Combined Authority), Susannah Hardyman MBE (Impetus - Backing the best), Shuab Gamote, Chris Smyth (Financial Times), and Ben Harrison (Work Foundation at Lancaster University).
